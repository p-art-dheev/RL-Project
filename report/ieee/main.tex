% ==================================================================== abstract
\begin{abstract}
Edge--cloud platforms must decide for every task whether to run it on an edge device, a nearby fog server or a distant cloud region. Nearby compute in India runs on a carbon-intensive grid, while clean grids are far away, so each placement trades deadlines against carbon. The base paper, MERSEM, plans whole jobs once per fifteen-minute epoch with an evolutionary search and a fixed weight between the two goals. We formulate task placement as a semi-Markov decision process and train PACE, a preference-conditioned policy that takes the trade-off weight as an input. Training starts with behaviour cloning of a carbon-aware heuristic and continues with proximal policy optimization on domain-randomised simulations, using a reward that judges each task against its own sub-deadline. At run time a proportional-integral controller tunes the weight to hold a service-level target, and a safety shield removes clearly late placements. In simulation on the base paper's dataset, PACE misses 1.3 percent of deadlines against 13.3 percent for MERSEM with 22 percent less dynamic carbon, stays ahead under load and failures, and generalises to three of four unseen setups. On a real testbed spanning Coimbatore, Mumbai and Stockholm, PACE missed fewer deadlines than MERSEM in all six paired runs and matched the strongest heuristic with 15 percent less carbon.
\end{abstract}

deep reinforcement learning, proximal policy optimization, multi-objective reinforcement learning, edge computing, carbon-aware scheduling, DAG scheduling

\section{Introduction}
A smart-city platform runs three kinds of work on shared infrastructure: camera \emph{safety alerts} that must be detected within one second, \emph{graph analytics} over the road network that need seconds, and \emph{batch analytics} that may take hours. The compute spans an edge (camera boxes in Coimbatore), a fog (AWS Mumbai, about 30\,ms away but on a grid of roughly 680\,\gco/kWh) and a cloud (AWS Stockholm, about 150\,ms away, roughly 35\,\gco/kWh). Every task placement trades latency against carbon, and the best choice changes with load, the hour's carbon intensity, the network and failures.

This is a natural reinforcement learning (RL) problem. Decisions are frequent and repetitive, so experience is plentiful. The best action depends on a joint state (queues, carbon, network, position in the job's DAG) that is hard to model by hand. The outcome is delayed: a job's deadline is only met or missed when its last task finishes. And the objective itself changes, because operators move between ``cheapest carbon'' and ``never miss an alert''. The base paper, MERSEM~\cite{mersem}, uses RL only to tune the mutation strength of an evolutionary search that is re-run every 15-minute epoch with a fixed weight between the two objectives.

We make the RL agent the scheduler itself. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A semi-Markov decision process (SMDP) for online, task-level DAG placement, with a \emph{deadline-decomposition reward} that assigns credit to individual tasks of large DAGs.
\item \textbf{PACE}, a size-agnostic, \emph{preference-conditioned} policy trained with behaviour cloning (BC) and proximal policy optimization (PPO)~\cite{ppo} under domain randomisation, so one network covers the whole deadline--carbon trade-off.
\item Deployment-time control: a proportional-integral (PI) controller that adapts the preference to an SLA target, and a safety shield.
\item An evaluation against a re-implementation of MERSEM and strong heuristics in simulation, on unseen setups, and on a real AWS testbed.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Reinforcement learning and PPO}
An agent in state $s_k$ takes action $a_k\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_k)$, receives reward $r_k$ and maximises the expected discounted return $J(\theta)=\mathbb E_\pi\big[\sum_k\gamma^k r_k\big]$. Policy-gradient methods ascend $\nabla_\theta J=\mathbb E\big[\nabla_\theta\ln\pi_\theta(a_k\mid s_k)\,A^\pi(s_k,a_k)\big]$, where $A^\pi$ is the advantage. PPO~\cite{ppo} stabilises this by clipping the probability ratio between the new and old policy, and generalised advantage estimation (GAE) trades bias for variance with a parameter $\lambda$. In a \emph{semi}-MDP, decisions occur at irregular event times rather than fixed steps.

\textbf{Multi-objective RL.} With two rewards (deadlines and carbon) and a preference $w\in[0,1]$, a \emph{preference-conditioned} policy $\pi_\theta(a\mid s,w)$ is trained over many $w$ so that a single network represents the whole Pareto front~\cite{abels2019,yang2019}. \textbf{Constrained RL} instead keeps one objective under a threshold and adapts a Lagrange multiplier, for example with a PID controller~\cite{stooke2020}.

\subsection{Base paper and related work}
MERSEM~\cite{mersem} schedules graph-analytics DAGs from the Rezaee--Adabi dataset~\cite{dataset} on an edge--fog--cloud system. Each epoch it minimises $w_s\,\mathrm{SLA}+w_c\,\mathrm{CO}_2$ ($w_s+w_c=1$) with an evolutionary algorithm, where tabular Q-learning picks how many jobs a local-search step reassigns. It places whole jobs on one machine, assumes exact runtimes, has no failures or priority classes, and is evaluated only in simulation. DeepRM~\cite{deeprm} showed online RL scheduling in a single cluster, and its follow-up added a BC warm start~\cite{deeprm2}. Decima~\cite{decima} scores DAG stages with a shared network, the idea behind our size-agnostic policy. Dependent-task offloading with deep RL~\cite{wang2021} ignores carbon, and temporal shifting~\cite{wiesner2021} motivates our deferral action.

\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Environment}
Nodes $n$ have $k_n$ execution slots, speed $s_n$ relative to a reference core, location $\ell_n$ and grid zone $z_n$. Job $j$ arrives at $a_j$ from device $o_j$ as a DAG with deadline $D_j$. Its tasks $i$ have work $W_i$, but the agent only sees the estimate $\hat W_i=W_i\epsilon_i$, $\ln\epsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$. Jobs come in three classes: \textbf{A} alerts (1\,s deadline; 30\% may not leave India; 98\% of jobs), \textbf{B} analytics and \textbf{C} batch, the last two being DAGs from the dataset. Moving $d$\,MB from location $a$ to $b$ takes
\begin{equation}
\tau_{ab}(d)=\tfrac12\mathrm{RTT}_{ab}+8d/B_{ab}, \label{eq:transfer}
\end{equation}
and executing task $i$ on node $n$ while $u$ of its slots are busy takes
\begin{equation}
T_{in}=\frac{W_i}{s_n}\Big(1+c_n\frac{u-1}{k_n-1}\Big), \label{eq:exec}
\end{equation}
with a contention factor $c_n$. The dynamic carbon of the task, following the Cloud Carbon Footprint power model~\cite{ccf}, is
\begin{equation}
C_{in}=\mathrm{PUE}_n\,\Delta P_n\,\frac{T_{in}}{3600}\cdot\frac{I_{z_n}(t)}{1000}\ \ \mathrm{[g]}, \label{eq:carbon}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta P_n$ is the power above idle and $I_z(t)$ the hourly grid intensity in \gco/kWh. The service-level (SLA) violation rate over class A and B jobs is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SLA}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal J_{AB}|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal J_{AB}}\mathbb 1[f_j>D_j], \label{eq:sla}
\end{equation}
with $f_j$ the time the result reaches the device. The operator wants low carbon subject to $\mathrm{SLA}\le\varepsilon$.

\subsection{Semi-Markov decision process}
\textbf{Decision epochs.} A decision is taken whenever a task becomes ready (all its parents finished), a deferred task wakes, or a failure orphans tasks. Tasks ready at the same instant are decided one by one with time frozen~\cite{deeprm}, so the action space grows linearly with the number of nodes.

\textbf{State.} $s_k=(x^{\mathrm t},\{x^{\mathrm n}_m\}_{m=1}^K)$. The task vector $x^{\mathrm t}\in\mathbb R^{20}$ holds the class, the estimated work and remaining critical path, the time budget $b_i$ to the task's sub-deadline (below), the slack ratio, deferral and privacy flags, the number of descendants, the recent miss rate, utilisation, time of day, two \emph{job-pressure} features (remaining job work relative to time left and to server capacity), and the preference $w$. Each of the $K$ candidate nodes has $x^{\mathrm n}_m\in\mathbb R^{18}$: tier, speed, the estimated transfer, waiting, execution and finish times relative to $b_i$, the carbon intensity now and its forecast change, the task's estimated carbon there, load, and whether the estimated finish meets $b_i$ and $0.4\,b_i$. The finish estimate is
\begin{equation}
\hat f_{in}=t+\max\big(\hat T^{\mathrm{in}}_{in},\hat T^{\mathrm{wait}}_n\big)+\hat W_i/s_n. \label{eq:finish}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Action.} $a\in\{1,\dots,K,\textsc{Defer}\}$. A mask $\mathcal M(s)$ removes failed nodes, nodes without memory, cloud nodes for privacy-restricted tasks, other devices' edge nodes, and \textsc{Defer} unless the task is class C with enough slack and fewer than three deferrals. Masking keeps every sampled action feasible, so the agent never wastes experience on invalid moves.

\textbf{Reward with deadline decomposition.} A job's outcome is known only at its end, and a large DAG has dozens of decisions, so a job-level reward cannot tell which placement caused a miss. We give each task its own sub-deadline, the job deadline minus the estimated remaining critical path after it: $\mathrm{lf}_i=D_j-\widehat{\mathrm{RCP}}_i$, with budget $b_i=\mathrm{lf}_i-t$. With the lateness penalty
\begin{equation}
\phi(f,d,h)=\mathbb 1[f>d]\big(1+\min(1,(f-d)/h)\big), \label{eq:pen}
\end{equation}
the reward of the decision that placed task $i$ is
\begin{multline}
r_i=-\Big[(1-w)\frac{C_{in}}{W_i\,\bar c_{\mathrm{fog}}}\\+\frac w2\,\phi(f_i,\mathrm{lf}_i,\mathrm{lf}_i-a_j)+\frac w2\,\phi(f_j,D_j,D_j-a_j)\Big], \label{eq:reward}
\end{multline}
where $\bar c_{\mathrm{fog}}$ is the carbon per unit of work on the fog, a normaliser that puts both terms on the same scale so that $w$ is meaningful. The task term attributes lateness to the decision that caused it, and the job term keeps the true goal in view. We discount per decision with $\gamma=0.8$: a placement mainly affects its own job.

\subsection{Policy network}
Fig.~\ref{fig:loop} shows the loop and Fig.~\ref{fig:net} the network. One MLP $g_\theta$ (two layers of 128 units) is applied to every candidate node with the task features appended: $h_m=g_\theta([x^{\mathrm n}_m;x^{\mathrm t}])$. Each node gets a score $z_m=v^\top h_m$, a small head scores \textsc{Defer} from $x^{\mathrm t}$, and
\begin{equation}
\pi_\theta(a\mid s,w)=\frac{\exp(z_a)\,\mathbb 1[a\in\mathcal M(s)]}{\sum_{a'\in\mathcal M(s)}\exp(z_{a'})}. \label{eq:policy}
\end{equation}
Because the weights are shared across nodes, the same network (about 60\,K parameters) handles any number of nodes, and a failed node is simply masked. The critic $V_\theta$ reads $x^{\mathrm t}$ and the max- and mean-pooled $h_m$. A decision takes about 0.55\,ms on one CPU core.

\subsection{Training: behaviour cloning, then preference-conditioned PPO}
\textbf{Behaviour cloning.} The agent first imitates the carbon-aware heuristic CA-EFT (Section~\ref{sec:setup}) for three iterations,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{BC}}(\theta)=-\mathbb E\big[\ln\pi_\theta(a^{\mathrm{CA}}\mid s,w)\big], \label{eq:bc}
\end{equation}
so that PPO starts from sensible behaviour instead of random placements, which miss most deadlines.

\textbf{PPO.} Each episode samples $w\sim U[0,1]$. With $\rho_k=\pi_\theta(a_k\mid s_k,w)/\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_k\mid s_k,w)$, GAE advantages $\hat A_k$ ($\lambda=0.9$) and returns $\hat R_k$, we minimise
\begin{multline}
\mathcal L(\theta)=-\mathbb E_k\big[\min(\rho_k\hat A_k,\ \mathrm{clip}(\rho_k,1\!-\!\epsilon,1\!+\!\epsilon)\hat A_k)\big]\\
+0.5\,\mathbb E_k\big[(V_\theta(s_k)-\hat R_k)^2\big]-\beta\,\mathbb E_k\big[\mathcal H(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_k))\big], \label{eq:ppo}
\end{multline}
with $\epsilon=0.2$, learning rate $3\cdot10^{-4}$, four epochs per iteration, gradient clipping at 0.5, and the entropy weight $\beta$ decaying from 0.01 to 0.002.

\textbf{Domain randomisation.} Each episode draws a new world: 2--6 edge, 1--3 fog and 1--3 cloud nodes, server speeds $\pm$20\%, RTT $\times$0.7--1.5, bandwidth $\times$0.5--1.5, alert rate 0.1--0.4 per camera per second, analytics load scaled to the server count, and estimate noise $\sigma\in[0,0.4]$. Jobs come only from the first 70\% of the dataset timeline; all results use the last 30\%. Algorithm~\ref{alg:train} summarises training (82 PPO iterations, about 3.5\,M decisions, 35 minutes on a laptop CPU).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Training PACE}
\label{alg:train}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE training jobs, CA-EFT teacher, iterations $N$
\STATE initialise $\theta$; minimise $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{BC}}$ \eqref{eq:bc} for 3 iterations
\FOR{$it=1$ \TO $N$}
  \FOR{each parallel worker}
    \STATE sample a randomised world and $w\sim U[0,1]$
    \STATE run $\pi_\theta$ for 6000 decisions
    \STATE when jobs finish, assign rewards $r_i$ by \eqref{eq:reward}
  \ENDFOR
  \STATE compute $\hat A_k$ by GAE; 4 epochs of minibatch steps on \eqref{eq:ppo}
\ENDFOR
\STATE \textit{(real testbed)} calibrate a digital twin and fine-tune $\theta$ in it
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Deployment: SLA-target controller and safety shield}
Because $w$ is an input, changing it changes the behaviour instantly, without retraining. After every ten completed class A/B jobs, with $\bar m$ the miss rate of the last 50 and the target $\varepsilon$,
\begin{equation}
e=\bar m-\varepsilon,\ \ I\leftarrow\mathrm{clip}(I+e,-5,5),\ \ w=\mathrm{clip}(w_0+K_pe+K_iI,\,0,1), \label{eq:pi}
\end{equation}
with $K_p=0.8$, $K_i=0.08$. This plays the role of a Lagrange multiplier on $\mathrm{SLA}\le\varepsilon$~\cite{stooke2020}: more misses raise $w$, headroom lowers it. A new target resets $I$ to avoid wind-up. The \textbf{safety shield} keeps only nodes with
\begin{equation}
\hat f_{in}-t\le(1-0.6\,w)\,b_i \label{eq:shield}
\end{equation}
and abstains if none qualifies; the policy still chooses among the remaining nodes.

% ==================================================================== V
\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}
\textbf{Simulator.} A discrete-event simulator implements \eqref{eq:transfer}--\eqref{eq:carbon} with transfer jitter, failures and stragglers; schedulers see only estimates. We use a \emph{testbed} topology (2 edge, 2 fog, 2 cloud nodes) and a \emph{city} topology (8/3/4). Carbon intensities are synthetic hourly profiles built from real annual means (Coimbatore 560, Mumbai 680, Stockholm 35\,\gco/kWh) with daily solar and evening patterns.

\textbf{Baselines.} EFT (earliest estimated finish), carbon-greedy, and CA-EFT, the strongest hand-made rule: the lowest-carbon node whose estimated finish meets $0.4\,b_i$, otherwise EFT. \textbf{MERSEM} is our re-implementation of the base paper (no code is public), with $w_s\in\{1,0.5,0\}$; we follow the paper where it is specific and are generous elsewhere (it sees the actual next-epoch arrivals, and failed or unplanned jobs fall back to EFT).

\textbf{Protocol.} All methods see the same jobs and carbon trace per seed; we use 10 seeds (5 for stress tests), held-out jobs only, and paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. PACE uses $w=0.9$ unless stated.

\subsection{Learning behaviour}
Fig.~\ref{fig:train} shows training. After cloning CA-EFT, PPO cuts validation carbon by about 40\% within 15 iterations (44\% by the end) while keeping alert misses around the teacher's level; the reward is noisy because every episode is a different random world and preference.

\textbf{Ablation.} Table~\ref{tab:ablation} isolates the RL components on the testbed topology. Behaviour cloning alone reproduces its teacher and \emph{ignores} $w$ (identical results at $w=0$ and $0.9$). PPO with the preference input turns $w$ into a real control: from $w=0$ to $0.9$ the misses fall from 11.5\% to 1.3\% while carbon rises only from 0.30 to 0.39\,g, and at $w=0.9$ PPO halves BC's carbon with fewer misses. Restricting PACE to MERSEM-style whole-job placement costs analytics deadlines (51\% missed), so task-level placement matters. The reward design mattered most: our first reward split the job penalty across tasks, and the policy ignored $w$ and missed 45\% of analytics deadlines; deadline decomposition \eqref{eq:reward} fixed this.

\subsection{Generalisation to unseen setups}
Without retraining, we tested setups outside the training distribution (Fig.~\ref{fig:e6}). Thanks to the shared scorer, a city twice the size works unchanged; so do a cloud moved to Frankfurt (a grid zone never seen) and twice the work with three times the data, where PACE beats both baselines in 10/10 seeds ($p=0.002$) with less carbon. It \emph{fails} on a much slower WAN: it keeps sending alerts to Stockholm, while CA-EFT simply recomputes finish times. Network conditions far outside training are the policy's main blind spot.

\subsection{Real AWS testbed}
The testbed has two laptop processes as one-core camera boxes in Coimbatore, and an x86 \texttt{c7i-flex.large} and an ARM \texttt{t4g.small} instance each in Mumbai and Stockholm. Workers run real tasks (OpenCV pedestrian detection and a BLAS kernel), and the orchestrator runs the \emph{same} scheduler code as the simulator. We measured node speeds, contention and network delays to build a calibrated \emph{digital twin}, and fine-tuned PACE in it with the same PPO procedure.

Running for real exposed three sim-to-real gaps: (1) the dispatch model ignored the controller's round trip; (2) two-vCPU x86 instances are one physical core, so concurrent tasks ran 1.7--1.8$\times$ slower; (3) the network to Mumbai slowed over the evening, so policies trusting the calibrated delay sent alerts there and missed them, and PACE lost to MERSEM in that first run. We fixed (1) and (2) in the twin and (3) with an online estimate that adds the 90th percentile of the last 30 measured delay residuals per region to every scheduler's estimate.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The RL agent's advantages come from three design choices: deciding per task when it becomes ready (robust to load and failures), conditioning on the preference (one policy, run-time control), and the deadline-decomposition reward (credit assignment in large DAGs). Its weaknesses are large analytics DAGs without the shield, because the reward lacks the cost a placement imposes on other jobs, and networks far outside the training range. Natural next steps are a graph neural network encoder over the DAG, a reward term for queueing delay caused to others, wider network randomisation with the online estimate inside training, and PPO-Lagrangian~\cite{ray2019} instead of the external PI controller.

Threats to validity: carbon intensities are synthetic profiles from real annual means (free hourly data for the Indian zones was unavailable); energy is modelled from CPU time because AWS exposes no per-VM power; the testbed is small (six nodes, three seeds per load); and MERSEM is a re-implementation, to which we were deliberately generous.

% ==================================================================== VIII
\section{Conclusion}
We cast edge--cloud task placement as an SMDP and trained a preference-conditioned PPO agent, PACE, with a deadline-decomposition reward, behaviour-cloning warm start and domain randomisation. One network covers the whole deadline--carbon trade-off and is steered at run time to an SLA target. PACE beats the base paper by an order of magnitude in missed deadlines in simulation with less carbon, generalises to most unseen setups, and on a real three-region testbed missed fewer deadlines than MERSEM in every paired run while matching the best heuristic with 15\% less carbon. Code and data: \url{https://github.com/p-art-dheev/RL-Project}.
